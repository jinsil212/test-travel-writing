On a Tuesday evening in August 2011 in a rented rehearsal space above a noodle shop in To Kwa Wan, a 60-year-old man picked up a guitar. He was jamming with friends, amateur stuff, cover songs for nobody. Halfway through a song, his left hand stopped moving. Then his legs gave out. His skull hit a concrete stairway on the way down and split open a 5-in gash above his left ear. By the time an ambulance arrived, the right side of his body was already dead. The man on that floor was Gordon Liu. He had played the most iconic Shaolin monk in cinema history. He had been Quentin Tarantino's white eyebrow killing master in Kill Bill. His face had been sampled, referenced, and worshipped by the Wu-Tang Clan for two decades, and he was about to disappear into a wheelchair, a courtroom, and a silence that has now lasted longer than his entire film career. His real name was Sin Kwok Wah, born in Foshan, Guangdong province in 1951, the same city that produced Wong Fei Hung, the most mythologized martial artist in Chinese history. That is not a coincidence. It is the whole story. His family moved to Hong Kong when he was a child. His father sold antiques and arts and crafts exports out of a shop in Sheung Wan. The household was comfortable. Gordon was the youngest of four children, spoiled and undisciplined by his own later admission. His parents wanted nothing to do with martial arts. They feared it would turn him into a street fighter. He started training anyway. Around age seven, he began sneaking off to a Hung Gar school run by a man named Lau Cham. Lau Cham was not a random Sifu. He was a direct student of Lam Sai Wing, who was the senior disciple of Wong Fei Hung himself. That makes the lineage four generations [music] deep. Wong Fei Hung to Lam Sai Wing to Lau Cham to the kid from Sheung Wan who kept cutting school. Between 15 and 20, Gordon skipped classes almost daily to train. Hung Gar tradition demanded up to three years of nothing but stance work before you learned a single technique. Gordon did three years of stance work. By night, he taught classes at Lau Cham's school. By day, he worked as a shipping clerk. Lau Cham's wife encouraged him to take the family surname. He became Lau Kar Fai, in the West, Gordon Liu. [music] He was not adopted, he was not Lau Cham's son, he was his godson. That distinction matters. Nobody chose him by default, he chose the lineage and the lineage accepted him. Lau Cham had two biological sons already working in film. The elder was Lau Kar-leung, a top action choreographer at Shaw Brothers Studios. The younger was Lau Kar-wing. Gordon became their godbrother and Lau Kar-leung was about to become the most important director he would ever work with. Shaw Brothers Studios in the mid-1970s was a factory. They shot on permanent backlot sets at Movie Town in Clearwater Bay. They produced dozens of martial arts films a year and they had just promoted Lau Kar-leung from choreographer to director. His first lead actor was Gordon Liu. The film was Challenge of the Masters in 1976. Gordon played Wong Fei-hung, the folk hero his own godfathers lineage had trained under. A real lineage descendant playing the ancestor on screen. Nobody in the audience knew that, but Lau Kar-leung knew it. He built the next decade of his career around Gordon's body. Over the following 12 years they made roughly 19 films together. Executioners from Shaolin, Heroes of the East, Dirty Ho, My Young Auntie, Martial Club, [music] Legendary Weapons of China. All four screenplays Lau ever wrote, starred or featured Gordon Liu. No director star partnership in kung fu cinema was more prolific and in 1978 they made the one that changed everything. The 36th Chamber of Shaolin was not the biggest grossing Shaw Brothers film. It earned close to 3 million Hong Kong dollars domestically, but it did something no kung fu film had done before. It spent more than 45 minutes inside a training sequence. Gordon plays Liu Yu-de, a young student whose family is killed by Manchu soldiers. &gt;&gt; [music] &gt;&gt; He flees to the Shaolin Temple, he shaves his head and then chamber by chamber he earns his way through 35 stages of physical and mental discipline. Wrist strengthening with a stone-tipped rod, balance drills on floating logs, headbutting sandbags until his skull bleeds. [music] A pole striking gauntlet that plays like a full-body percussion exam. Each chamber has its own logic, its own failure condition, [music] its own moment of breakthrough. The training sequence is the movie. Gordon carries every frame of it alone. No sidekick, no love interest, no comic relief until the final act. He plays the same character at three ages and makes each one physically distinct. The young [music] student is loose and panicked. The mid-training monk is tense and methodical. The graduated master San Te moves like water finding its level. He shaved his own head for real. That image, bald skull in a yellow robe, became the most recognizable silhouette in kung fu cinema. It won Best Martial Arts at the 24th Asian Film Festival. American distributors retitled it Master Killer and dumped it into grindhouse theaters in 1979. One of those grindhouse theaters was on 42nd Street in Manhattan. A kid from Staten Island named Robert Diggs saw it there when he was about 14. [music] He had already seen it once on Channel 5 at age 9. He would eventually watch it roughly 300 times. He built an entire mythology around it. He named his crew after another Gordon Liu film. He called his debut album Enter the Wu-Tang: 36 Chambers. The opening track samples a line of dubbed English dialogue from Shaolin and Wu-Tang, a 1983 film that Gordon Liu directed himself. The line about Shaolin shadowboxing and the Wu-Tang sword style became one of the most recognized audio samples in hip-hop history. Robert Diggs became RZA. His crew became the Wu-Tang Clan. And Gordon Liu, the actor whose dubbed voice launched the whole thing, never received a credit, a royalty, or a phone call. The Wu-Tang Clan named their neighborhood Shaolin. They named their chambers, they named their styles, all of it traced back to a Cantonese actor working on a back lot in Clearwater Bay. Gordon Liu's face is on bootleg t-shirts in every Chinatown in America. His voice opens one of the best-selling rap albums of the 1990s, and almost nobody wearing those shirts or playing that album could tell you his [music] name. That Then came July 7th, 1983. Alexander Fu Sheng, Shaw Brothers biggest young star, died in a car accident. He had been the lead of a film still in production called The Eight-Diagram Pole Fighter. Lau Kar-leung rewrote the script overnight. Gordon Liu's character, Fifth Brother Yang, became [music] the protagonist. There was a scene in that film where Gordon shaves his head on camera. It was always in the script, but after Fu Sheng's death, it became something else. It became grief filmed in real time. The Eight-Diagram Pole Fighter is widely considered Lau Kar-leung's darkest and most brutal film. It was also the last great martial arts picture Shaw Brothers ever produced. The studio stopped making films in 1986, and when the factory closed, Gordon Liu had no factory. Jackie Chan had already gone international. Jet Li was rising fast on the mainland. Sammo Hung and Yuen Biao were building their own production pipelines. Gordon Liu signed with TVB and spent the 1990s doing television, police procedurals, supporting comedic roles, steady work that nobody outside Hong Kong ever saw. In 1993, a film called Last Hero in China put him on a rope bridge opposite Jet Li. The scene plays like a passing of the torch, old-school Hung Gar against new-school wushu, the bald Shaw monk against the rising mainland idol. The fight is good, the metaphor is brutal. That same year, Lau Kar-leung was hired to choreograph Jackie Chan's Drunken Master II. The two men [music] clashed over fight philosophy so severely that Lau walked off the production before the final fight was shot. Chan reshot it in his own style. Lau responded by making Drunken Master III with Gordon Liu. It flopped, [music] a master and his godbrother answering a slight with a box office disaster. The 1990s were not kind. Then, in 2002, a phone call came from Los Angeles. Quentin Tarantino wanted Gordon Liu for a film called Kill Bill. Liu later told CNN he had barely heard of the director. His own words in a 2007 interview captured the confusion perfectly. He was a 1970s Asian star. He could not understand why anyone would want him in a 21st century American film. They met in Shanghai. Gordon wore a formal Chinese suit. [music] Tarantino, to his surprise, wore one, too. The first word Tarantino said to him was Shifu, master. For volume one, Gordon played Johnny Mo, the masked leader of the Crazy 88. Tarantino had him kill and be killed in the most elaborately choreographed sword fight in American film history. For volume two, something stranger happened. Tarantino had originally cast himself as Pai Mei, the white-eyebrowed Shaolin villain. He trained for the role. He put on the costume. He started shooting. Gordon Liu watched him in the makeup chair and told him to his face that he looked like Santa Claus, not a martial arts master. Tarantino has confirmed the story. He said, "It seemed like a big pain to play the character and he wanted to concentrate on directing." He said having Gordon Liu in the wings made the decision easy. He called it almost criminal not to cast him. The Pai Mei that ended up on screen, stroking his beard, flicking his wrist, humiliating Uma Thurman with a contemptuous grin, &gt;&gt; [music] &gt;&gt; was Gordon Liu's creation. The beard-stroking gesture was his idea. It was not in the script. And here is the detail that makes it ache. In Executioners from Shaolin, made in 1977, Gordon Liu played the hero who fights Pai Mei. 26 years later, he became the villain he once tried to kill. Kill Bill made Pai Mei iconic. It did not make Gordon Liu a star. He returned to Hong Kong. He did a Bollywood crossover film in 2009 called Chandni Chowk to China, playing a smuggler villain opposite Akshay Kumar for Warner Brothers. He attended a documentary premiere in Rome. He reunited with Lau Kar-leung for a final collaboration. And he kept playing guitar with friends in a rented room in Tokwawan until August 2011, when a stroke dropped him on a staircase and opened a wound that has never fully closed. The paralysis took his right side. The speech impairment took his voice. Within 18 months, he had lost nearly 50 lb. Visitors to the hospital found a man who could nod, shake his head, and move his left arm. That was it. When a reporter asked if he was happy, he nodded. When a visitor's zipper was down, Gordon spotted it immediately and tried to gesture. Sharp mind, locked body. His second wife, Ma Fei Fung, and their two children were estranged by then. Tabloid reports alleged they had pried open floorboards in his Cane Road apartment searching for hidden cash. Ma denied it publicly, saying the floors were being repaired for mold damage. Gordon filed for divorce in June 2012. He entrusted his remaining liquid assets, roughly 2 million RMB plus a US dollar account, to his assistant of 5 years, a woman named Eva Fung. By December 2012, the money was gone and Eva was not returning calls. Gordon Liu could not speak, could not walk, and could not get his own savings back. His elder sister Teresa, living in the United States, arranged for actress Amy Fan, a long-time friend, to take over as legal guardian in January 2013. Amy paid for lawyers out of her own pocket. She hired a private nurse. She moved Gordon into a nursing home. And she went after Eva Fung in court. Eva initially refused even to accept the subpoena. The confrontation reportedly ended with Amy Fan shouting at her outside a building demanding she take the paper threatening to call police. Eva took it. The Bank of China account showed 1.39 million RMB. The HSBC account showed 58,000 US dollars. On April 29th, 2014, 1 day before the High Court hearing, Eva agreed to return approximately 1.2 million RMB plus interest. Gordon Liu, the man who had played the most disciplined monk in cinema, the most terrifying master in Tarantino's universe, got most of his money back through a last-minute settlement negotiated by a television actress and funded by her personal savings. Two months before that settlement, on June 25th, 2013, Lau Kar-leung died at Union Hospital in Hong Kong. He was 76. Lymphoma had stalked him for two decades. When Amy Fan told Gordon the news, his eyes went red. He did not speak. He &gt;&gt; [music] &gt;&gt; never speaks when he is unhappy. The man who made him, who named him, who [music] pointed a camera at him 19 times and built the most important kung fu film ever shot around his body was gone. [music] And Gordon could not say goodbye out loud. In 2020, the Martial Arts History Museum inducted him into its Hall of Fame. In February 2025, he appeared at the Hong Kong Stunt Men Association's New Year dinner in a wheelchair, receiving a contribution award. It was his first significant public appearance in years. He is 73 years old. He lives in a Hong Kong nursing home under Amy Fan's guardianship. He does not give interviews. He has not acted since 2011. The paradox of Gordon Liu is simple to state and impossible to resolve. His image is everywhere. His bald head in that yellow robe is printed on shirts sold in cities he has never visited. His dubbed voice opens one of the most important albums in hip hop. His white eyebrows and contemptuous grin define one of Tarantino's most quoted characters. Hundreds of millions of people have consumed his work. Almost none of them know his name. Bruce Lee died young and became a god. Jackie Chan survived and became a brand. Gordon Liu survived and became a sample. He is sitting in a nursing home in Hong Kong right now. A fourth generation inheritor of Wong Fei Hung's kung fu, the monk, the master, the man whose voice built a mythology he was never invited into. Still sharp behind the eyes. Still silent.
